There's a scene late in *Norwegian Wood* where Watanabe climbs alone to his dormitory roof at night, carrying a firefly his roommate handed him in a jar as a goodbye gift. He worries it's already dying, opens the lid, and watches it take a long, faltering time to lift off and vanish into the dark — thinking, the whole time, about Naoko, far away and just as dimmed. The connection is widely made between that scene and something real: the fireflies released every summer at a garden a short walk from the dorm where Murakami actually lived as a student. You can stand in that same garden on a summer evening and watch the same thing happen.

Haruki Murakami's 1987 breakthrough follows Toru Watanabe through a realist, grounded late-1960s Tokyo — a rarer register for an author better known for surreal detours. Its two real backdrops are just as concrete: the Waseda dormitory Murakami lived in himself, and a mountain road north of Kyoto that traces, as far as anyone's confirmed it, the route to the sanatorium where Naoko is treated.

## Stop 1: Wakeijuku and Waseda University, Tokyo — where the firefly scene began

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ee/Waseda_University_-_Okuma_Auditorium_-.JPG" alt="Okuma Auditorium at Waseda University in Tokyo" credit="XIIIfromTOKYO" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Waseda_University_-_Okuma_Auditorium_-.JPG" />

Watanabe's dormitory and his "Storm Trooper" roommate are drawn directly from Wakeijuku, the real all-male dorm in Mejirodai where Murakami lived for six months in 1968 after moving to Tokyo for Waseda. Its south dormitory still looks close to how it did then, and Munatsuki Hill, a short walk away, is a place Murakami has named in his own essays — he once fell down it, drunk, on his way home.

A short walk from there, in the neighboring Sekiguchi area, is Hotel Chinzanso, whose Japanese garden has hosted an annual firefly-viewing evening every early summer since 1954 — the likely real origin of the story that grew into this novel, which makes the book's most-remembered scene, for once, traceable to an actual place rather than an invented one.

Back on Waseda's main campus, the ivy-covered Building 2 — Watanabe's fictional refuge when he can't sort out his feelings for Naoko and Midori — still stands, now home to the Aizu Museum and Takata Memorial Library.

- **Getting there**: Waseda Station (Tokyo Metro Tozai Line) or Takadanobaba Station (JR Yamanote Line, Seibu Shinjuku Line), both a short walk to the main campus; Hotel Chinzanso is closer to Edogawabashi Station (Tokyo Metro Yurakucho Line).
- **Good to know**: Chinzanso's firefly evenings run mid-May through late June, after dark — check the hotel's own site for the current year's dates, since public tickets are limited and sell out.

## Stop 2: North of Kyoto — the road toward Naoko's sanatorium

Naoko is treated at a fictional sanatorium called Ami Hostel (阿美寮) for a breakdown she can only ever describe to Watanabe in fragments. He reaches her by a bus from Kyoto's Sanjo that follows the Kamo River north — and that much of the route is real and ridable today, the same city bus line still running up to Ohara as the streets thin out and the river narrows. Past Ohara is where the novel's own geography turns deliberately vague, and where the confirmed trail runs out: Murakami never names a real building behind Ami Hostel, and readers have argued for decades over which remote spot he had in mind, from a monastery hamlet in Kyoto's northern Kitayama hills to the mountain temple Bujo-ji near Miyama.

None of these is confirmed, and that's arguably true to the book — Ami Hostel is deliberately unlocatable. What's real is the first leg: riding that same bus and watching Kyoto give way to mountains is the closest the novel gets to a place you can actually visit.

- **Getting there**: From Sanjo or Kyoto Station, city bus (Ohara-bound, roughly an hour) follows the Kamo River north to Ohara — this is as far as public transit goes. Reaching any of the further candidate sites means a car and a full day.
- **Good to know**: This is a scenic ride more than a pinpoint destination — there's no marked "Ami Hostel" site to visit at Ohara or beyond. Go for the landscape described in the book (mountain road, river, isolation) rather than expecting a specific building.

## Practical travel notes

- **Best time to visit**: Waseda and Chinzanso year-round, though the firefly evenings only run mid-May to late June; the Kyoto mountain route is best avoided in heavy winter snow.
- **Base yourself in**: central Tokyo for Waseda and Chinzanso; Kyoto city for the Ohara bus ride.
- **The book itself**: the standard English translation is Jay Rubin's, published by Vintage International.
