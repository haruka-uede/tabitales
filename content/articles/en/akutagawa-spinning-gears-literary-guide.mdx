Walk north on Ginza's main street at dusk and you can feel it too: the low-grade dread Ryunosuke Akutagawa gives his narrator in *Spinning Gears* (歯車, *Haguruma*, also translated as *Cogwheels*) — an ordinary evening crowd that suddenly seems too cheerful, too unaware of whatever is pressing in on him.

Akutagawa wrote it between March 23 and April 7, 1927, months before he took his own life that July. It's barely disguised autobiography: the narrator is never named, but the hotel he keeps returning to, the bookstore where a poster catches him off guard, and the transparent, spinning gears that keep interrupting his sight are all drawn directly from Akutagawa's own final months.

This guide follows the short, walkable route at the center of the story — the hotel where he stayed that spring, the street he paced through in growing unease, and the bookstore where the visions caught up with him again.

## Stop 1: The Imperial Hotel, Hibiya — where the gears first appeared, and where his own crisis came to a head

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ac/Imperial_Hotel%2C_Tokyo.jpg" alt="The Imperial Hotel Tokyo in Hibiya, Chiyoda, Tokyo" credit="Ons" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Imperial_Hotel,_Tokyo.jpg" />

*Spinning Gears* opens with the narrator dashing by car and train from a countryside resort to reach Tokyo in time for a friend's wedding reception. Walking the last stretch toward the hotel, tired and irritable, he notices something at the edge of his vision: a half-transparent, spinning gear, the first of the hallucinations that recur through the rest of the story.

He checks into a room and keeps returning to it across several unraveling days — in the story, drafting the very account we're reading in between short trips out into the city. It's in this room that he learns his brother-in-law has been killed, struck by a train while wearing a raincoat out of season. That raincoat is one of the story's own inventions: newspapers at the time reported the real man was found with an overcoat, not a raincoat. Akutagawa's real brother-in-law did die exactly this way that January — struck by a train, suspected of arson and insurance fraud, six months before Akutagawa's own suicide — but the raincoat itself seems to be a detail his mind supplied on its own, which may be exactly why it keeps coming back to haunt the narrator for the rest of the story.

The hotel's real hold on Akutagawa that spring went further than the fiction lets on. In April and May of 1927 — the same weeks he was finishing this story — he twice tried to arrange a lovers' suicide pact at the Imperial Hotel with Masako Hiramatsu, a friend of his wife's acting as his secretary. Both times she changed her mind and fled before anything happened.

The narrator never names the hotel, but it's identified as the Imperial Hotel — at the time, still Frank Lloyd Wright's dramatic 1923 building. That structure is gone now; it was demolished in 1968, and its striking main entrance was dismantled and rebuilt at the Meiji-mura architecture museum in Inuyama, Aichi, far from Tokyo. The Imperial Hotel itself never closed, though — a later building has stood on the same Hibiya site ever since, across the street from Hibiya Park.

- **Getting there**: Hibiya Station (Hibiya, Chiyoda, and Mita lines), about 2 minutes on foot; also a short walk from Yurakucho or Ginza stations.
- **Good to know**: One scene plays out right at this entrance — the narrator, arriving back after visiting a mental hospital, finds a man in a raincoat arguing with the hotel valet and turns around without going inside, spooked by the coincidence.

## Stop 2: Ginza-dori — the street he paced through in the fading light

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/06/Chuo-dori_Street_from_Ginza_4-Chome_Crossroads_%28south%29.JPG" alt="Chuo-dori, Ginza's main shopping street, seen from the Ginza 4-chome crossing" credit="そらみみ (Soramimi)" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chuo-dori_Street_from_Ginza_4-Chome_Crossroads_(south).JPG" />

Restless in his room, the narrator heads out to Ginza's main street as evening falls, walking north through the crowd. The shops and passersby around him seem untouched by guilt or dread, which only deepens his own. He ducks into a bookstore piled with magazines, picks up a children's book of Greek myths almost at random, and one line inside it hits him hard enough to stop him where he stands — though even Akutagawa never tells us what the line said.

Ginza-dori — officially Chuo-dori along this stretch — is still Tokyo's best-known shopping street, though department stores and flagship boutiques have long since replaced the small bookshops of the 1920s. The story never names the exact shop, so treat this as the real street behind that walk rather than a single marked storefront.

- **Getting there**: Ginza Station (Ginza, Marunouchi, and Hibiya lines) sits right on the street; Higashi-Ginza Station is a couple of minutes' walk away, near Kabukiza.
- **Good to know**: On Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays, Chuo-dori through Ginza closes to cars and becomes a pedestrian-only "hokosha tengoku" from noon to 6pm (April–September) or noon to 5pm (October–March) — about as close as a modern visitor gets to walking this street the way Akutagawa did.

## Stop 3: Maruzen Nihonbashi — where the gears came back

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/37/Nihonbashi_Maruzen_Tokyu_Building_2026-03-15.jpg" alt="The Nihonbashi Maruzen Tokyu Building, home of Maruzen's Nihonbashi bookstore" credit="Asanagi" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nihonbashi_Maruzen_Tokyu_Building_2026-03-15.jpg" />

Maruzen is the one place in *Spinning Gears* Akutagawa names outright. Its celebrated red-brick Nihonbashi building, with a second-floor foreign-books hall, had opened in 1910 — but it burned down in the fires that followed the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake, and Maruzen didn't complete a permanent replacement until after the war. Whatever stood on the site in April 1927, the narrator pulls August Strindberg's *Legends* off a shelf there and finds it unsettlingly close to his own experience; the Swedish playwright wrote it during his own bout of paranoid delusion. He then opens a German-published book of psychiatric patients' artwork and finds a full page of figures with gears instead of faces, echoing exactly what keeps intruding on his sight.

Picking up a copy of *Madame Bovary* — a novel he's already read many times over — he recognizes himself in its bored, provincial husband. Wandering into the store's print gallery afterward, one poster catches him off guard: a knight spearing a winged dragon. The Japanese word for dragon, *ryu* (龍), is the same character buried in Akutagawa's own given name, Ryunosuke (龍之介) — a coincidence the narrator can't shake.

Maruzen has run a Nihonbashi bookstore continuously since it opened here in 1870, though the building itself has been rebuilt more than once since Akutagawa's visit, including after wartime bombing. Its current store, inside the Nihonbashi Maruzen Tokyu Building, still sells books a few minutes' walk from Nihonbashi Bridge.

- **Getting there**: Nihonbashi Station (Tozai, Ginza, and Toei Asakusa lines), directly connected to the building.
- **Good to know**: The story has the narrator step out afterward onto an already-quiet, dark Nihonbashi-dori — today the street stays lively well into the evening, so go expecting ordinary foot traffic rather than the empty road in the text.

## Practical travel notes

- **Best time to visit**: Any season works. The roughly 2.7km walk from Hibiya through Ginza to Nihonbashi takes well under an hour at an easy pace, tracing something close to the narrator's own route. Go on a weekend afternoon if you also want to catch Ginza's car-free hours.
- **Base yourself in**: Central Tokyo — all three stops sit within about a 20-minute walk of Tokyo Station, close enough to combine into a single afternoon rather than a dedicated trip.
- **Language**: English signage is standard throughout central Tokyo; nothing about this route needs special preparation.
- **The book itself**: the standard English translation, titled *Spinning Gears*, is by Jay Rubin, collected in *Rashomon and Seventeen Other Stories* (Penguin Classics), with an introduction by Haruki Murakami. It has also been published as *Cogwheels*, translated by Cid Corman and Susumu Kamaike.
