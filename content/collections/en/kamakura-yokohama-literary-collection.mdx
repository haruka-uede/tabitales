Kamakura and Yokohama sit on the same stretch of coast southwest of Tokyo, a short train ride apart — and between them, they hold real settings from three very different novels.

This route follows the coast from Kamakura's Enoden line into Yokohama's old foreign quarter, all reachable as a single long day trip from Tokyo, or a more relaxed overnight.

## Stop 1: Koyurugi-misaki, Kamakura — the cape behind *No Longer Human*

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Koyurugi_Cape_and_Koshigoe_Fishing_Port_%28Feb_2022%29.jpg" alt="Koyurugi Cape and Koshigoe fishing port, Kamakura" credit="SuFlyer" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Koyurugi_Cape_and_Koshigoe_Fishing_Port_(Feb_2022).jpg" />

The pivotal scene in Dazai Osamu's *No Longer Human* has its narrator attempt suicide with a bar hostess on the Kamakura coast. Dazai drew this directly from his own life: in 1930, at 21, he attempted the same thing at this small cape with a 19-year-old bar hostess, who died. He survived.

Koyurugi-misaki is still there today — a quiet point between Shichirigahama and Koshigoe beaches, with a shrine at its tip and a view toward Enoshima. Full details: [Following *No Longer Human* to Tokyo and Kamakura](/articles/dazai-no-longer-human-literary-guide).

## Stop 2: Yuigahama Beach, Kamakura — where *Kokoro* begins

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/24/Kamakura_Yuigahama_Beach_2024.jpg" alt="Yuigahama Beach, Kamakura" credit="Klaasjeoranje" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kamakura_Yuigahama_Beach_2024.jpg" />

Yuigahama Beach, a short ride away on the same Enoden line, is the mainstream real-world identification for the opening scene of Soseki's *Kokoro*, where the narrator first notices the man he'll come to call Sensei.

It pairs naturally with Kamakura's temples and shrines. Full details, plus *Kokoro*'s Tokyo-side setting: [Following *Kokoro* to Kamakura and Tokyo](/articles/soseki-kokoro-literary-guide).

## Stop 3: Yamate, Yokohama — the hillside Tanizaki lived out in *Naomi*

<StopImage src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4e/Ehrisman_house_2024.jpg" alt="The Ehrismann Residence in Yamate, Yokohama" credit="Kakidai" creditUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ehrisman_house_2024.jpg" />

From Kamakura, it's roughly half an hour by train to Yokohama, where Junichiro Tanizaki's 1924 novel *Naomi* closes in the old foreign settlement on the Yamate bluff — the same neighborhood Tanizaki himself moved to in 1922, taking up the ballroom dancing and Western dress he later gave his characters.

A cluster of preserved Western-style houses, centered on the Ehrismann Residence, is open to the public for free. Full details: [Retracing *Naomi* to Yokohama's Foreign Quarter](/articles/tanizaki-naomi-literary-guide).

## Practical travel notes

- **Order and pacing**: Koyurugi-misaki and Yuigahama are both on the Enoden line within Kamakura, combined with a short ride on the same line; Yokohama is a separate onward leg, roughly 30 minutes by train from Kamakura. Treat this as either one full day covering all three, or Kamakura and Yokohama as two separate half-day trips.
- **Best time to visit**: Kamakura gets crowded on summer weekends; a weekday or off-season visit is quieter for both beaches. Yamate's gardens are especially pleasant in spring.
- **Base yourself in**: Tokyo, with all three stops reachable as day trips — standard IC cards (Suica/Pasmo) work throughout.
